**Key points, in order**

2. **What you should see or identify**
On the lateral wall, the tympanic membrane is a semi-transparent oval membrane that separates the external and middle ear. The malleus attaches at the umbo, a concavity in the central part of the membrane [Lecture 16b - Middle Ear > II. External Ear > 7. Tympanic Membrane (ear drum)]. On the medial wall, you should be able to identify the oval window (with the stapes footplate) and the round window [Lecture 16b - Middle Ear > III. Middle Ear (Tympanic Cavity) > 3. External Ear > 7. Tympanic Membrane (ear drum)]. The line concerned the effect of a damaged tympanic membrane on hearing. Q: What nerves supply the muscles of the hand?
====================================================================================================
**Where this fits**
This is the Hand region, Lecture 09, under Nerves of the Hand [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve] [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve].

**Key points, in order**
1. Median nerve: it enters the hand through the carpal tunnel [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve].
2. Its recurrent branch supplies the thenar muscles, except the deep head of FPB [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve].
3. Its digital branches give motor branches to lumbricals 1 and 2 [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve].
4. Ulnar nerve: it enters the hand through the ulnar canal, between the pisiform and the hook of the hamate and medial to the ulnar artery [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve].
5. It gives branches to the hypothenar eminence [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve].
6. Its deep branch runs with the deep palmar arch and supplies the interossei, adductor pollicis and the deep head of FPB [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve].
7. The ulnar nerve supplies the majority of the hand's muscles [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve].

**What you should see or identify**
Identify the median nerve passing through the carpal tunnel and giving off its recurrent branch to the thenar eminence [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve]. Identify the ulnar nerve between the pisiform and hook of hamate, with its deep branch accompanying the deep palmar arch [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve].

**What goes wrong / clinical relevance**
- Carpal tunnel syndrome comes from increased pressure in the carpal tunnel. Pressure causes degeneration of the median nerve and loss of function [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve].
- Median nerve injury causes loss of the lumbricals to digits 2 and 3 and progresses to a claw deformity of those two digits only ("median nerve claw") [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve].
- Ulnar nerve injury proximal to the hand causes extensive loss of hand function and "ulnar nerve claw". The claw is more pronounced in digits 4 and 5 (loss of lumbricals and interossei) and less pronounced in digits 2 and 3 (loss of interossei only) [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve].
- A line in the median nerve notes about thenar loss after injury is currently under review [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve]. General anatomy knowledge, pending Dr. Inglis: only an injury at or proximal to the recurrent branch (for example at the wrist/carpal tunnel or higher) causes thenar loss. A lesion distal to the recurrent branch spares the thenar muscles, and nerve lesions can recover depending on severity.
- A line in the median nerve notes about the claw/benediction mechanism is currently under review [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve]. General anatomy knowledge, pending Dr. Inglis: the benediction sign with a forearm/elbow-level median lesion is due to loss of FDS/FDP to digits 2 and 3 when making a fist, not lumbrical loss. The median claw is due to lumbrical loss with unopposed MCP extension.
- A line in the ulnar nerve notes about the superficial branch is currently under review [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve]. General anatomy knowledge, pending Dr. Inglis: the motor branches to the 3rd and 4th (medial) lumbricals come from the deep branch, not the superficial branch. The superficial branch is mainly sensory plus palmaris brevis.

**What to look at next**
Next in the lecture is Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Ulnar Nerve [Lecture 09 - The Hand > Nerves of the Hand > Median Nerve].

Q: What does the stapedius muscle do?

**Key points, in order**
1. The stapedius muscle attaches to the stapes bone, the smallest ossicle. [Lecture 16b - Middle Ear > III. Middle Ear (Tympanic Cavity) > 2. Major structures include]

4. It is the smallest skeletal muscle in the human body. It arises from the pyramidal eminence and inserts on the stapes. [Lecture 16b - Middle Ear > III. Middle Ear (Tympanic Cavity) > 2. Major structures include]
5. On the posterior wall of the middle ear, it attaches to the stapes, increases tension and dampens vibration of the oval window membrane. [Lecture 16b - Middle Ear > III. Middle Ear (Tympanic Cavity) > 3. Walls of the Middle Ear]

**What you should see or identify**
The stapedius muscle on the posterior wall of the middle ear, arising from the pyramidal eminence and attaching to the stapes. The footplate of the stapes closes the oval window on the medial wall. [Lecture 16b - Middle Ear > III. Middle Ear (Tympanic Cavity) > 3. Walls of the Middle Ear]

Q: What is the superficial fascia of the anterior abdominal wall like?
====================================================================================================
**Where this fits**
This is the superficial fascia of the anterolateral abdominal wall, covered in Lecture 16 [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 05]. The lecture works through the wall layer by layer, from superficial to deep [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06]. Lecture 15 also notes that these layers continue into the urogenital triangle [Lecture 15 - The Perineum > Urogenital Triangle > Subcutaneous Perineal Fascia].

**Key points, in order**
1. The superficial fascial component has a loose layer and a dense layer [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 05].
2. Camper's fascia lies just beneath the skin. It is adipose connective tissue, typically yellow, and highly variable in thickness [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06].
3. Camper's fascia is nearly absent in frail elderly individuals and can be very thick in obese individuals. This contributes to android obesity ("beer belly") [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06].
4. Camper's fascia insulates against heat loss and cushions the internal organs against mechanical trauma [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06].
5. Scarpa's fascia lies just deep to Camper's. It is dense irregular connective tissue, so its thickness is more consistent. It anchors Camper's fascia to the underlying musculature, like "elastic wallpaper paste" [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06].
6. Below the abdomen, Camper's fascia continues into the urogenital triangle as subcutaneous fatty tissue, and Scarpa's fascia continues as the perineal (Colle's) fascia [Lecture 15 - The Perineum > Urogenital Triangle > Subcutaneous Perineal Fascia].

**What you should see or identify**
Identify a yellow, fatty Camper's layer just under the skin, then a deeper, more consistent, dense Scarpa's layer [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06]. Because your cadaver has more fat and less muscle, expect Camper's fascia to be on the thicker side, since the notes say its thickness varies and can be remarkably thick in obese individuals [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06]. Scarpa's fascia should stay fairly consistent in thickness [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06].

**What goes wrong / clinical relevance**
The notes link a thick Camper's fascia to android obesity ("beer belly") [Lecture 16 - Anterolateral Thoracoabdominal Wall > Slide 06]. No other clinical consequences are stated in the sources.

**What to look at next**
